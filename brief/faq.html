Questions, answered honestly

The questions we get — and our straight answers.

Including the limits. Tap any question to open it.

What is this, in one line?
A two-sided coherence platform: a free, private wellness onramp for people — a "mirror and navigator" that helps someone find their way to care, then lets them go — and a paid integration offramp for organizations. A measurement layer (KCI) keeps both pointed at the same thing: the person.
How is this different from every other wellness app?
The incentive is inverted. Most apps win when you stay logged in — they're built to capture attention and data. Ours is built to get you where you're going and then let you go ("find-and-release"). Nothing is harvested, no one is trapped. That's not something a capture-economy competitor can copy without gutting their own model.
If you're not tracking people, how do you know it's working?
Two ways that never touch identity: anonymous counts (how many reached out, how many connected — on indicators the organization defines) and a voluntary "the Bridge sent me" handoff — when a person chooses to enter care, they simply say it, and the connection gets tallied. Reach is measured; privacy is never touched.
Where does the data actually go?
Nowhere by default. Everything a person types stays on their own device — no account, no login, nothing transmitted. The organization only ever sees anonymous counts — never names, never words.
Is this compliant? What about 42 CFR Part 2 / HIPAA?
The private interaction creates no protected health record — nothing private is collected, stored, or sent — so the strict substance-use confidentiality rules (Part 2) don't attach to it. That's being outside the rule's scope by design, not circumventing it. The organization-facing side runs in a separate, HIPAA-aware environment, built to fit existing protocols rather than replace them.
What if the AI gives a diagnosis or misses a crisis?
It can't, by design. The companion is non-diagnostic — it reflects feelings and asks open questions; it never assesses clinical risk or labels a condition. And crisis routing is hard-wired to humans (988 / 911 / the helpline), never judged by a model. The rule across the whole system: the AI proposes; a deterministic safety layer disposes. The worst case is always the safe case.
Is this just another dashboard my team has to babysit?
No — the offramp's job is to remove grind, not add a tool to monitor. It keeps a person's journey whole across the seams between programs, so staff aren't re-doing intake at every handoff. The point is to give a stretched workforce time back, not another screen.
What's actually behind the AI?
A coordinated team ("guild") of specialized AI agents — led by Claude, with focused roles and lighter "edge" models for routine work — coordinating over a shared knowledge graph so a person's journey stays one continuous thread. It runs regular coherence self-checks to stay aligned to its purpose. It is not sentient, not a therapist, not a diagnostic tool — it's a system organized to take grind off humans and reduce the disorder that keeps people from care that already exists.
What's KCI — and isn't "you can't game it" just a slogan?
Key Coherence Indicators measure the health of the whole system instead of isolated parts. The design property: a participant shouldn't be able to raise the score by making another stakeholder worse off — so the incentive to "win" by draining another department goes away. (It's patent-pending; the formula and implementation are protected, so we keep those out of materials like this.) Plain-English: when you measure the parts, the parts compete; when you measure the whole, the parts cooperate.
What are you actually measuring?
Four pillars: Reach (are the people who never knock on the door connecting?), Coherence (does a person's pathway stay whole across programs?), Capacity (is grind coming off scarce staff?), and Equity (measuring for disparity, not just totals, so the hardest-to-reach aren't hidden behind aggregate numbers).
Who are you actually trying to reach?
The "95%" — the majority who aren't seeking care. About 81% of people with a substance use disorder get no treatment in a year, and ~93% of them don't because they don't think they need it. These aren't people refusing help; they're people the system was never designed to find — often driven into hiding by the all-or-nothing "addict vs. fine" story.
How is it free for people and still sustainable?
The two sides fund each other: free for the people who need it, sustained by the organizations that serve them. That's the intersection where everyone wins — and it's why the consumer side can stay genuinely free and private.
Where does this go beyond one app?
The same idea extends to keeping a person's pathway whole across the wider public-service web — justice, child welfare, benefits, perinatal care — the flagship example being someone leaving custody who needs to land in treatment before they fall back. Connecting that web depends on consent coherence (the right information moving only with the right permission). This is direction, not a shipped feature. We never let the vision outrun what's actually true.
What do you NOT claim?
It's not therapy, not a diagnosis, not a crisis service, and not sentient AI. It's not deployed at scale yet — it's proposed as a no-PHI, no-procurement pilot. And we don't promise outcomes we can't measure: we can improve the conditions, the communication, and the time spent — we can't guarantee human behavior, and we say so.

Honest by design — including about the limits. If a question isn't here, it's worth asking.
