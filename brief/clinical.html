What R95 actually asks of a system

R95 ("Reaching the 95%") is built on a simple inversion: design for the ~95% who aren't seeking treatment, not just the ~5% who are. In practice that means lowering barriers (disconnecting "readiness" from total abstinence), meeting people where they are, and communicating worth and compassion regardless of where someone is in their change.

A low-barrier approach doesn't replace abstinence-based care — it encompasses it. People's goals shift day to day, and the system has to hold them through that rather than expelling and re-admitting them. The hard part has never been the philosophy. It's reaching the 95% and knowing, honestly, whether you did.

The idea

Coherence: take the grind off the human, at every level.

The system doesn't fail for lack of clever parts. It fails because the parts don't cohere — each is optimized in isolation while the whole loses people. The same failure repeats at every level: the person's brief window of readiness, the helpline agent, the clinician, the data team — a scarce, irreplaceable human consumed by grind a machine should carry.

We build a layer that takes that grind off the human and measures whether everyone is better off — patient, agent, clinician, analyst. We call it Key Coherence Indicators (KCI). Its defining property: you cannot raise the score by making any one stakeholder worse off.

This is also how we keep the AI aligned. KCI doesn't just measure the system — it points the technology at the people it's meant to serve. Because the only way the score rises is everyone being better off, the AI can't optimize for clicks, throughput, or any proxy at a stakeholder's expense. The same rule keeps the human and the machine focused on the same thing: the patient — not the metric.

It also keeps the whole system tied to its stated mission — so front-line reality and leadership intent stay connected, and the right call becomes the obvious one.

How KCI works, briefly

Most systems measure parts — beds filled, calls closed, dollars spent — and the parts quietly compete. KCI measures the whole: is information flowing without friction, is the time from reach-out to care shrinking, is anyone being left behind? It's a single score with an anti-extraction property baked into the math — a gain that comes at another stakeholder's expense lowers the total. (Subject of a provisional patent filed 2026-03-24.)

We don't sell AI — anyone can claim AI. We sell coherent implementation: making it actually work inside a real system. That's the hard part, and it's our part.

Security, privacy & confidentiality

Private by design — not by promise.

The most common question first: if a person's data stays on their phone, how do you measure reach? Here's the whole answer in one picture.

42 CFR Part 2, in plain terms — and why it doesn't attach to the Bridge

Part 2 is the federal rule that gives SUD records special protection. The 2024 Final Rule (compliance required February 16, 2026) aligned much of it with HIPAA — a single consent can now cover treatment/payment/operations, redisclosure rules eased for HIPAA-covered entities, and "SUD counseling notes" got psychotherapy-note-level protection. Across the field, the hard problem is that information exchanges largely rely on "trust, not verification" for consent, and front-line staff can't reasonably manage every nuance by hand.

The Bridge sidesteps the whole question: it creates no Part 2 record. No identity, no SUD record, nothing transmitted — so Part 2 simply doesn't attach. And when we extend into provider-facing tools (a separate, HIPAA-lane build), we design them Part 2-aware from the start — consent-management as a first-class feature, not an afterthought. We speak this language fluently; it's a feature, not a footnote.

The AI, with guardrails

Safe by default — AI as an option, never a risk.

The companion runs in Safe Mode by default: deterministic, on-device, and built so a crisis is always routed to a real human — never judged by AI. Richer AI is opt-in, and every tier keeps the same hard guardrails.

Tier 0
Safe Mode (default, live). Deterministic + local. Accepts correction, never mislabels, never diagnoses, deterministic 988/911 crisis routing. The pilot runs on this.
Tier 1
On-device AI. Richer conversation that still never leaves the phone. Same crisis override, same no-diagnosis rule.
Tier 2
Cloud assist — optional, opt-in, no PHI. Off by default; not used in the pilot. Shown only to demonstrate the ceiling.
How we prevent the things that worry people (hallucination, mislabeling, crisis)

Crisis is never AI-judged. A fixed, deterministic check routes any sign of danger straight to 988 / 911 / the Helpline. No model decides whether someone is safe.

No mislabeling, and corrections are accepted. If a person says "I didn't say I was angry," the companion apologizes and stops — it never insists on an interpretation the person rejected. This behavior is covered by an automated regression test suite so it can't quietly regress.

No diagnosis, ever. Medical/symptom questions get supportive routing to a real person — never an assessment or a dose.

The rule across every tier: the AI proposes, the deterministic safety layer disposes. When AI is added, its output is re-checked by the same Safe-Mode guardrails before anything is shown. Worst case is always Tier-0 behavior.

What this opens

The same idea, across the system.

The Bridge is the first of these — the same move (a specialized agent that gives a scarce human their time back) fits everywhere the system loses people:

  • Teams kept aligned — when a change ripples across units, it too often reaches the people who must execute it late, creating rushed turnarounds and friction. A coherence layer keeps everyone working from one current picture — and gives leadership an accurate, real-time read on how the system is functioning.
  • Connection data, kept true — bed/provider availability stays current and feeds the network picture instead of fighting it.
  • Provider communication — keep policy, guidance, and support coherent between the county and its providers.
  • Analytics, freed — let the data team use the data to improve the system, instead of wrangling it.

Each is a separate conversation — the point is that the pilot proves the pattern.

Built to fit

Aligned with California's behavioral health transformation.

Designed to slot into where the system is already going — not to add another silo:

  • A no-wrong-door front door — low-barrier, and no diagnosis required to get help, matching the early-intervention focus.
  • Reaches the priority populations — people with complex SUD needs, the unhoused, and the uninsured / non-Medi-Cal-eligible, not only those already in care.
  • Culturally and linguistically responsive — multilingual by design (Spanish first, then the full supported set).
  • Outcomes, transparency, and disparity reduction — measured honestly, in the system's own language, with privacy intact.
What it lets you decide

A clinician doesn't order a test they won't act on.

So everything here is built around decisions you can actually make — not data for its own sake:

  • Where the system is losing people — a beneficiary-side signal of the motivation window you've never been able to see, so you can act upstream of the drop-off.
  • Whether a change is working — a clean, pre-registered continue / expand / stop at 60 days, in your own metrics. No sunk cost, no ambiguity.
  • Where to put scarce capacity — what to take off your team's plate, so the people you can't hire spend their time where only people can.

Each one is a decision, in plain language — the numbers serve the call, not the other way around.
